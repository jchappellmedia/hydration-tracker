# Application Analysis — HVAC Service Technician

## Fit: Weak on this specific posting. The career change itself is workable. This job is not the door in.

I built the materials with the career-change framing you asked for, and the resume is genuinely good for what it is. But I would be doing you a disservice if I let you send this without saying the obvious thing.

**The posting requires three years of HVAC experience. You have none.** Not adjacent, not partial, none. And unlike a soft "8+ years preferred" line, this one is load-bearing. A service technician's actual job is diagnosing why a compressor is short-cycling or why a system is icing over. No amount of transferable skill covers that. A company handing you a truck, a fuel card, and unsupervised residential calls needs someone who already knows.

The posting also says they use **AI tools to review resumes and identify "potential inconsistencies or verification signals."** So padding is both wrong and unusually likely to get flagged here.

Realistic odds on this specific listing: low. Send it anyway if you want — it costs an hour and occasionally a shop is desperate enough to train. Just do not build a plan around it.

## What is genuinely strong in your favor

Everything on the resume is true and it is a better case than most career changers bring:

- **Customer service is your standout.** 5.0-star reviews for over a year. Service companies lose more money to techs who are rude at the door than to techs who are slow, and every hiring manager in the trades knows it.
- **Self-directed field work.** Six years scheduling your own jobs, driving to sites, and finishing them alone. That is the actual daily shape of this job minus the HVAC knowledge.
- **You are comfortable on job sites.** Years filming construction, trades, and industrial work means ladders, attics, roofs, and heat are not going to surprise you. Most career changers from an office have never been in a 140-degree attic in July.
- **Equipment stewardship.** $30k inventory at ALA, $1M of gear at GCE. "Maintain inventory on service vehicle" is a listed duty and you have a real answer for it.
- **Teaching.** "Assist other Service Technicians with questions" — you did that professionally for two years.
- **Physical requirements and driving record.** Straightforward clears.

## The money, plainly

$28/hr is about **$58,240 a year**. Your stated floor is **$75,000**. This is roughly **$17,000 below it**, and the $2,500 sign-on bonus closes that gap for about seven weeks.

And that is the *top* of the entry range. If you come in without experience, a shop that takes a chance on you would likely start you as a helper or apprentice at **$18–$22/hr**, which is $37k–$46k. Career changes into the trades usually cost two to three years of reduced income before journeyman rates catch up.

That is not an argument against doing it. Plenty of people make that trade deliberately and are happier for it. But you are currently holding queued applications at $100k–$250k in media, and the gap between those two paths is large enough that it should be a decision you make on purpose, not one that happens because a posting looked interesting on a bad day.

## The actual path in, if you want this

If the trades are a real direction rather than a passing thought, here is the sequence that works:

1. **EPA 608 Certification.** Federally required to handle refrigerant. Roughly $50–$150, self-study, and you can test in a few weeks. **Get this first.** It is the single cheapest thing that moves you from "no experience" to "entry-level candidate," and many shops will not look at you without it.
2. **Apply to Helper / Apprentice / Installer roles, not Service Technician.** Search "HVAC apprentice," "HVAC helper," "HVAC installer no experience." Those postings say no experience required and exist specifically for people in your position. Install crews are the normal entry point — you ride along, carry equipment, learn the systems, and move to service in one to two years.
3. **Consider a program.** Mesa Community College and East Valley Institute of Technology both run HVAC programs. Some Valley shops also run paid apprenticeships that cover schooling.
4. **Your Part 107 and business background are real assets later.** A tech who can run a business is a future Field Maintenance Manager, which this posting names as a growth path.

## Bottom line

The resume is honest and it is the best version of your case. The odds on this posting are low because the three-year minimum is real. Get the EPA 608 certification and aim at apprentice and helper postings instead — that is where a career change into HVAC actually starts, and you would be a strong candidate for those tomorrow.

Before any of it, be clear-eyed about the pay. This path costs real money for a few years. Worth it for some people; worth knowing either way.
