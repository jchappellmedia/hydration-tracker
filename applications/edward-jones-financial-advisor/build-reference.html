Role Basics (standard Edward Jones advisor program)

  • Company: Edward Jones — one of the largest brokerages in the U.S., ~19,000 advisors, branch-office model (one advisor + one branch office administrator per office).
  • Role: Financial Advisor (entry level / career changer track)
  • Model: You build your own book of clients in your community. Edward Jones provides the office, the BOA, training, and licensing sponsorship.
  • Licensing: They sponsor and pay for SIE, Series 7, and Series 66 exams plus state insurance licenses. Study time is paid.
  • Compensation shape: Salary plus bonus during training and ramp-up (typically the first 4–5 years, on a declining schedule), transitioning to commissions/asset-based earnings. New advisor first-year total comp commonly lands in the $75K–$100K range depending on market and performance.
  • No finance experience required. They recruit career changers, business owners, salespeople, teachers, and military veterans.

What the Role Actually Is

  • Prospecting: networking, community involvement, referrals, and outreach to build a client base from scratch.
  • Meeting with individuals and families to understand goals, then recommending investments and insurance.
  • Ongoing client service and relationship deepening.
  • Running your branch like your own business, with accountability to activity and asset goals.

What They Screen For

  • Drive and resilience (the first 3 years are a grind).
  • Relationship and communication skills.
  • Entrepreneurial history — business owners are their favorite profile.
  • Community ties in the market you'd serve.
  • Integrity (fingerprinting, background check, credit check — FINRA registration requires all three).

Company research

Edward Jones is a large U.S. brokerage and financial advisory firm run as a partnership, headquartered in the St. Louis area. It uses a branch-office model: usually one financial advisor and one branch office administrator per office, serving individuals and families in their own community. It hires a lot of career changers for its new-advisor program. The firm pays for licensing and training, and pays a salary that steps down over the first several years as commissions and asset-based pay grow. All employee ratings below cover the whole company (advisors, branch staff and home-office staff together). None of them are specific to Mesa or the East Valley.

Glassdoor

3.5 / 5 (about 5,300 (5,316-5,318 depending on snapshot)) Company-wide rating, a little below the 3.7 average for financial services employers. Reviewers like running their own office and schedule, the training, supportive coworkers, the conservative investing approach and the firm's stability. Common complaints: heavy door-knocking in the first couple of years (one reviewer says about two hours a day), long hours with little support early on (one says 80 hours a week for the first five years), strict accountability for sales numbers, a big pay gap between associates and General Partners, and a recent shift toward wealthier clients. The review pages that came up mix very old reviews (2008-2009) with 2026 ones, so some of these opinions may be out of date. Source

What employees say: pros and cons

Pros

  • Paid training and licensing for people with no finance background. Postings describe trainee pay, plus a supplemental salary for up to five years that steps down as commissions and asset-based pay grow. [1] [2] [3]
  • Ranked No. 21 on the 2026 Fortune 100 Best Companies to Work For list (No. 24 in 2025). The ranking is based mostly on employee surveys, though these figures come from the firm's own press releases. [1] [2] [3]
  • A lot of independence: reviewers say you run your own office and set your own schedule, like being your own boss within limits. [1] [2]
  • Several reviewers praise the training. One recent reviewer said they were surprised by how much training new advisors get (others called it high-level). [1]
  • Stable, privately held partnership with profit sharing and a possible path to partnership. Postings also list bonuses, paid parental and medical leave, and a travel award program. [1] [2]
  • Reviewers mention a client-first, conservative investing approach and coworkers who support each other. [1] [2]

Cons

  • The first years are mostly prospecting. Reviewers describe heavy door-knocking (about two hours a day for the first couple of years) and long hours with little support. One says plan on 80-hour weeks for the first five years. [1] [2]
  • Real sales pressure: reviewers say you are held strictly to your sales numbers, and trade press has covered the firm raising production expectations. [1] [2]
  • Many new advisors wash out. Research cited by InvestmentNews says about 71% of new advisors across the industry leave the profession within five years. Edward Jones advisor exits rose in 2025, and Q2 attrition went from 5.3% to 6.4%. [1] [2]
  • Reviewers point to a large pay gap between regular associates and General Partners. [1] [2]
  • Discrimination and retaliation lawsuits: a class action by former Black advisors over the client-transfer policy, a suit over the GoodKnight bonus program, a retaliation claim and a suit from a branch staffer. The firm denies the claims. [1] [2] [3]
  • Lower scores for job security, advancement and management on Indeed (3.2 and 3.3), and some reviewers feel the firm is shifting toward wealthier clients. [1] [2]

Watch for

  • Several discrimination and retaliation lawsuits are pending. These are allegations, and the firm denies them.
  • Advisor attrition rose in 2025 (Q2 attrition was 6.4%, up from 5.3%). The firm says this was mostly retirements.
  • A St. Louis Post-Dispatch headline says Edward Jones began layoffs, including at headquarters, but the date and size could not be verified from the search result.
  • Ratings are company-wide and mix very old reviews (2008-2009) with recent ones. None are specific to the Mesa / East Valley branches.

Bottom line

This is a real, well-known firm with a structured path for career changers: paid licensing, paid training, and a salary that phases out over about five years. Employee ratings are about average (3.5 on Glassdoor, 3.7 on Indeed, company-wide). The job itself is building a sales practice from scratch, with heavy prospecting, long early hours and many people washing out. It fits Joshua's business-owner background if he goes in expecting a hard first few years. Talking to a local East Valley advisor first is the best way to check whether it is right for him.
