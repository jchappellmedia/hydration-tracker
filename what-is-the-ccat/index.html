What is the CCAT test?

The CCAT is a 50-question test with a 15-minute limit. That's about 18 seconds per question. Employers use it early in hiring to see how quickly you solve problems you haven't seen before.

CCAT stands for Criteria Cognitive Aptitude Test. It's made by a company called Criteria Corp, and it's one of the most common tests you'll get asked to take after applying for a job. If a recruiter just sent you a link with a deadline, this is probably what you're facing.

What's actually on it

The 50 questions are mixed together, not grouped by type:

SectionAbout how manyWhat you'll see
Verbal18Analogies, opposites, sentence completion, spotting matching details
Math & logic21Word problems, percentages, ratios, number patterns, basic logic puzzles
Spatial11Shape patterns, odd-one-out, grids where you pick the missing piece

The rules that catch people out

  • You can't skip and come back. Once you move on, that question is gone for good.
  • Wrong answers don't count against you. So never leave one blank — always guess.
  • No calculator. The math is meant to be done in your head or on paper.
  • It gets harder as you go. Early questions are the easy points.
  • Almost nobody finishes. Under 1% answer all 50. Not finishing is normal.

Who uses the CCAT?

Thousands of employers, across almost every industry. It shows up most often for sales roles, analyst and operations jobs, software and technical roles, and management positions. Some private equity firms require it across all the companies they own, which is why you might run into it more than once during a job hunt.

How is it scored?

Your score is simply how many you got right, out of 50. The average is about 24. Employers usually compare your score to a benchmark for the role rather than a universal pass mark — so there's no single passing score. We break this down properly in the CCAT score guide.

Can you prepare for it?

Yes. The test is designed to measure aptitude, but a big part of what makes it hard is simply that it's unfamiliar and fast. Once you've seen the question types, you stop burning seconds working out what's being asked. That's why people's scores usually jump between their first and third practice test — nothing about them changed except familiarity.

Three things worth doing before test day:

  • Take one full timed practice test so the clock isn't a surprise.
  • Review what you got wrong — that's where the score actually moves.
  • Practice mental math, since that's where most people lose time.

Test day tips

  • If a question isn't clicking in about 20 seconds, guess and move on.
  • Have scratch paper and a pen ready before you start.
  • At the halfway mark (7:30), you want to be near question 25.
  • Rule out obviously wrong options — a 1-in-3 guess beats a 1-in-5 one.
